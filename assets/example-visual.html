Stuff wears out. We spread the cost over the years it's used.
One truck, bought today
The cost, split across 5 years
The truckCosts $50,000 today
The yearsUsed for 5 years
Each year$10,000 counted as cost
The catchNo new cash leaves the bank
Why it matters: profit looks smaller each year even though nothing new was paid, because the truck is quietly "using itself up."

The story

  1. Buy the truck for $50,000. Cash leaves once, on day one.
  2. The truck will do useful work for about 5 years.
  3. So each year, $10,000 shows up on the books as an expense.
  4. After 5 years the truck is fully "used up" on paper, even if it still runs.

Don't confuse it with

Depreciation isSpreading a cost you already paid across the years it helps you
It is notCash leaving the bank, or what a buyer would pay for the truck today

Where you'll see it

  • The P&L: a depreciation expense line lowering profit each year
  • The balance sheet: accumulated depreciation shrinking the truck's book value
  • Every EBITDA calculation: it gets added right back, because no cash moved
